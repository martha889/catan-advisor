# catan-advisor

A research project on what wins at the board game *The Settlers of Catan*. It has three parts:
- a rule-based **strategy engine**
- a **Monte Carlo lookahead** that plays candidate moves out into the future
- a **self-play simulator** that measures strategy ideas over tens of thousands of games

It also includes a browser tool that suggests moves for a game you're playing against bots.

> **For education and research only.** Use it to study strategy, review your own finished games, or play against bots. Don't use it for live help in games against other people. See [Disclaimer](#disclaimer).

## Key findings

All numbers come from simulated 1v1 games to 15 points with a 9-card discard limit (see [Limitations](#limitations)).

1. **Longest Road plus Largest Army is nearly decisive.** Players who end the game holding both win 99% of the time. Players holding neither win 2%.
2. **Your opening matters a lot.** Each production pip your two starting settlements have over your opponent's is worth roughly 3–4 points of win rate. A 4-pip edge wins 68% of games. On one recorded board, the opening alone moved the simulated win rate from 37% to 49%.
3. **Ore and wheat drive wins once you can build roads.** Openings with 13+ pips of ore and wheat win 56.5%, against 47% for 5–8 pips. Extra wood and brick beyond about 7 pips adds little.
4. **Save for cities and settlements.** The biggest single improvement came from not spending cards meant for the next city or settlement on roads or dev cards (62% against the original strategy).
5. **Winners buy dev cards.** Winners bought 7.1 dev cards per game, against 3.2 for losers, and played twice as many knights.
6. **Going first is worth about 7–8 points of win rate:** the first player wins 57.6% of games.
7. **Ports are overrated as opening spots.** Starting on a port wins 47%, slightly below average, because port spots produce less. Valuing ports by your *surplus* production is neutral; valuing them more aggressively loses games.

## Data analysis

Generated by `node sim/analyze.js 20000 500000`: 20,000 games of the current engine playing itself.

These figures describe how games go when this engine plays itself. They are correlations, not proof of cause and effect. They show what winning positions look like, and the [experiments](#strategy-experiments) test which choices actually cause wins.

### Overview

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Game length (turns, both players) | median 73, middle 80%: 57–92 |
| First player wins | 57.6% |
| Loser's final score | 8.8 VP on average |

### Where the points come from

| Source | Winner (avg VP) | Loser (avg VP) |
|---|---|---|
| Settlements | 2.83 | 1.97 |
| Cities (2 each) | 7.93 | 5.52 |
| Longest Road (2) | 1.56 | 0.43 |
| Largest Army (2) | 1.31 | 0.27 |
| VP cards | 1.49 | 0.64 |

Cities provide over half of a winner's points. A typical winner has 4 cities, so all city pieces are used. The gap between winner and loser is about 6 points: roughly 2.4 from cities, 2.2 from the two awards, 0.9 from settlements and 0.9 from VP cards.

### Longest Road and Largest Army

| Holding at the end | Players | Win rate |
|---|---|---|
| Both | 9,093 | 99.1% |
| Longest Road only | 10,817 | 61.2% |
| Largest Army only | 6,776 | 60.7% |
| Neither | 13,314 | 1.9% |

The two awards together are worth 4 points, more than a quarter of the 15 needed. In 1v1, whoever holds neither has almost no route to 15.

### What winners do differently

| Per game | Winner | Loser |
|---|---|---|
| Settlements built | 4.79 | 2.73 |
| Cities built | 3.97 | 2.76 |
| Roads on the board | 11.0 | 8.4 |
| Dev cards bought | 7.05 | 3.21 |
| Knights played | 3.04 | 1.50 |
| Bank/port trades | 12.5 | 6.9 |
| Cards lost to 7s | 5.6 | 5.1 |
| Turns with nothing built | 19.2 | 24.5 |

Winners build almost twice as many settlements and buy twice as many dev cards. They also trade with the bank almost twice as often. They lose slightly *more* cards to 7s, because their higher production means bigger hands.

### Openings: what predicts a win

By how many more production pips your two starting settlements have than your opponent's:

| Pip advantage | Players | Win rate |
|---|---|---|
| ≤ −4 | 3,080 | 31.6% |
| −3 to −2 | 7,639 | 40.0% |
| −1 to +1 | 18,562 | 50.0% |
| +2 to +3 | 7,639 | 60.0% |
| ≥ +4 | 3,080 | 68.4% |

By ore + wheat pips in the opening (cities and dev cards):

| Ore + wheat pips | Players | Win rate |
|---|---|---|
| 0–4 | 401 | 41.9% |
| 5–8 | 15,483 | 46.9% |
| 9–12 | 19,434 | 51.1% |
| 13+ | 4,682 | 56.5% |

By wood + brick pips in the opening (roads and settlements):

| Wood + brick pips | Players | Win rate |
|---|---|---|
| 4–6 | 6,161 | 48.0% |
| 7–9 | 19,699 | 50.1% |

The engine almost never opens with under 4 pips of wood + brick, so that range is missing here. When it did, in a real game, the result was a long stall with no way to expand.

By resources covered and by starting port:

### Case study: one opening, replayed

The board from a recorded 1v1 game was replayed from the same position: the opponent's first settlement fixed, and 500 games for each opening.

| Opening | Simulated win rate |
|---|---|
| Opening actually played: Ore 6 · Wheat 5 · Wood 11 + Sheep 8 · Wheat 4 · Brick 3 (only one wood source) | 36.8% |
| Best found: Wood 6 · Wheat 9 · Sheep 2 + Ore 6 · Wheat 4 · Brick 3 (all five resources) | 48.6% |
| Worst found: leaves the strong Ore 6 corner to the opponent | 2.6% |

The spots you *leave* your opponent matter as much as the ones you take. This led to the simulated-openings feature (below).

## Strategy experiments

Each change was measured head-to-head against the version just before it, on the same boards and dice. Results are shown as win rate ± a 95% confidence interval. The gains don't simply add up.

### What helped

| Change | Win rate vs. previous version |
|---|---|
| Don't spend cards meant for the nearest city or settlement on other things; score both opening spots as a pair; value roads less | ~62% vs. the original |
| Weight search: a victory point worth less relative to production; costlier bank trades | 51.7% ± 0.8 |
| Turn lookahead: play the top 4 moves out 10 turns over 16 dice sequences, and switch only on a clear gain | ~53% ± 1.7 |
| Simulated openings: play whole games out from each of the top 6 spots, 60 games each | 55.4% ± 2.4 |
| Play knights to race for Largest Army whenever it's winnable | 51.0–51.3% ± 0.8 |

### What didn't help

| Idea | Result |
|---|---|
| Trade toward a goal whenever nothing is buildable | hurt (about −3 points) |
| "Endgame mode": count production for less near the target | neutral |
| Rank goals by estimated turns to afford them | neutral |
| Value ports by total production | hurt (44.8%) |
| Value ports by surplus production | neutral at a moderate weight (kept); 31% at a high weight |
| Play every knight just to block the opponent | neutral |
| Discount a second building on the same tile (robber risk) | neutral |
| Penalize openings with little wood and brick | neutral (the engine already avoids them) |
| Push for expansion once every settlement is a city | slightly harmful |
| A deeper lookahead (24 sequences, 12 turns) | no better than the lighter one |

The search also confirmed that most weights are near their best: changing any one of them alone rarely moves the win rate by more than 1–2 points.

## Using the advisor in games against bots

The repo includes a browser tool (`index.html`) for 1v1 games: you against one bot. It runs entirely in your browser, with nothing to install and no account.

1. **Open it.** Clone or download the repo and open `index.html` in Chrome, Safari or Firefox.
2. **Load the board.**
   - **From a screenshot:** take a screenshot of the board (on macOS, ⌘⇧⌃4 copies one to the clipboard) and press ⌘V on the page. Or drop an image file onto it. Then click the number token on the **top-left** tile, then the one on the **bottom-right** tile. The tool reads every tile's resource and number.
   - **Or by hand:** use any board and fix each tile in the editor.
3. **Check the board.**
   - Tiles with a dashed outline were uncertain: click one to set its resource or number.
   - Click a port to change its type.
   - A green check means the tile and number counts match a standard board.
4. **Settings.** Choose who places first, the points to win (for example 10 or 15) and the discard limit, then press **Start game**.
5. **Record the game as it happens.**
   - **Setup:** click corners for settlements and edges for roads, for both players, in the order the game uses.
   - **Each turn:** enter the dice roll with the number buttons.
   - **Builds:** click a corner for a settlement (click your own settlement to upgrade it to a city) and an edge for a road.
   - **Robber, steals, dev cards and bank trades** have their own buttons. Use ± in the Hands table for anything else, such as trades with the bot.
   - **Undo** (⌘Z) reverts a misclick. Progress is saved in your browser.
6. **Read the advice.** The orange panel suggests your setup spots and road, your build order (including bank and port trades), where to put the robber, what to discard, and when to play dev cards. Gold markers show it on the board.

The browser tool uses the rule-based engine. The lookahead and simulated openings are in `lookahead.js` and used by the simulator; they haven't been wired into the browser tool yet.

## Reproducing the results

Requires Node.js 18 or later; there are no dependencies to install.

```sh
# Self-play statistics (the Data analysis section): 20,000 games, ~30 s on 16 cores
node sim/analyze.js 20000 500000

# One match: the current engine (B) against the frozen original strategy (A)
node sim/simulate.js 2000 7

# The current engine against itself with one weight changed
node sim/simulate.js 2000 7 --vs current spendReserve=10

# Large parallel check, with turn lookahead and simulated openings switched on
node sim/verify.js 1600 990000 lookahead=4,16,10 opening=6,60

# Search each weight up and down; keep changes that win at least 52%
node sim/search.js 3200 2
```

`verify.js` also accepts `A_TUNE='{"knightEager":6}'` to test against a different opponent style. Results vary slightly with the seed. With 16,000 games the 95% margin is about ±0.8 points.

## How the engine works

| Path | What it is |
|---|---|
| `engine.js` | Board geometry, rules bookkeeping and the strategy heuristics. Strategy weights live in `TUNE`. |
| `lookahead.js` | Monte Carlo search on top of the engine: simulated openings and turn-level lookahead. |
| `sim/` | Self-play tools: `simulate.js` (one match), `analyze.js` (statistics), `search.js` (weight search), `verify.js` (large checks), `engine-baseline.js` (the frozen original strategy). |
| `index.html`, `app.js`, `vision.js` | The browser tool and its screenshot reader. |

- **Opening:** scores both starting spots as a pair: production with diminishing returns, plus bonuses for being able to build roads (wood and brick) and cities (ore and wheat). `lookahead.js` can replace this score with simulated win rates.
- **Turn plan:** lists every affordable action (settlement, city, road toward a target, dev card), with the bank or port trades it needs. Each one is valued in "weighted pips", and the plan picks greedily, charging for trades and for anything that delays the nearest city or settlement.
- **Opponent awareness:**
  - defends Longest Road if the opponent could catch up, counting a possible Road Building card
  - values settlements that cut the opponent's road
  - races for Largest Army
  - estimates hidden VP cards
  - warns about Monopoly when you hold many of one resource
- **Robber:** blocks the opponent's most productive tile, weighted toward the leader, and avoids your own tiles.

## Limitations

- **The data is self-play.** The engine mostly played against itself or its earlier versions. Real players, especially strong humans, play differently, so findings about *this engine's* games may not carry over exactly.
- **No player-to-player trading.** The simulator models bank and port trades only.
- **Standard base game only:** a random board with standard tiles and numbers, 1v1. No expansions or 5–6 player rules.
- **The browser tool is 1v1 and manual.** You record both players' moves; the opponent's hand is tracked from what you enter.
- **Hidden information.** The simulator lets the engine see the opponent's hand when deciding a Monopoly; real games don't.
